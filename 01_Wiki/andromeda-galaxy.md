# Andromeda Galaxy

The nearest large galaxy to the Milky Way and the most distant object visible to the naked eye — a faint smear in Andromeda at magnitude 3.44, ~2.537 million light-years away. It is approaching us at ~110 km/s. In approximately 4.5 billion years, Andromeda and the [[milky-way|Milky Way]] will merge into a single elliptical galaxy. The collision has already begun.

## Resolving the Great Debate

In 1920 the "Great Debate" pitted Harlow Shapley against Heber Curtis: were the spiral nebulae (including Andromeda) clouds within our galaxy, or separate "island universes" — entire galaxies in their own right? Shapley argued within; Curtis argued outside.

Edwin Hubble resolved it in 1924 by identifying **Cepheid variable stars** in Andromeda — stars whose pulsation period directly encodes their intrinsic luminosity. By comparing apparent to intrinsic brightness he derived a distance of ~1 million light-years (later refined to 2.537 million), placing Andromeda far beyond any possible stellar cloud. The universe of galaxies was established.

## The Coming Collision

Andromeda is approaching the Milky Way. Simulations show the sequence:

1. **~4.5 billion years from now** — first close pass; the two galaxies interpenetrate. Stars are not dense enough to collide directly, but gravitational tides severely distort both galaxies.
2. **~6 billion years** — the galaxies separate briefly, then fall back together.
3. **~7–8 billion years** — the cores merge. A giant elliptical galaxy forms — sometimes called **Milkomeda** or **Milkdromeda**.

During the merger the Sun will almost certainly survive, but may be scattered to a very different orbit — possibly ejected to the outskirts of the merged galaxy. The night sky in the far future would look radically different: an intense band of stars from the colliding cores, no familiar spiral arms.

The collision is not catastrophic for stars. The average distance between stars is so large that direct stellar collisions are negligibly rare — the entire event is a gravitational reorganisation, not a physical impact.

## The Double Nucleus

Andromeda has a puzzling **double nucleus** — two concentrations of old stars near its centre. The leading explanation: a disk of old stars on elliptical orbits around the central black hole appears as two peaks depending on orbital phase. The actual black hole sits at the fainter, bluer concentration. This structure has no clear analogue in the Milky Way.

## Mythology

Andromeda was an Ethiopian princess, daughter of King Cepheus and Queen Cassiopeia. Cassiopeia boasted that Andromeda was more beautiful than the Nereids; Poseidon punished this hubris by sending the sea monster Cetus to ravage the coast. The oracle demanded Andromeda be sacrificed: she was chained to a sea cliff.

Perseus — returning with Medusa's severed head — saw her, killed Cetus, and married her. The entire mythological ensemble is preserved in the sky: Andromeda, Perseus, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, and Cetus are all constellations, clustered in the same region of the northern sky. Andromeda's galaxy lies within the constellation bearing her name.

## Satellite Galaxies

Andromeda's two brightest satellites are visible in binoculars:
- **M32** — compact elliptical, unusually dense; may be the stripped remnant of a once-larger galaxy
- **M110** — diffuse dwarf elliptical, one of the first objects Messier catalogued alongside M31

The Andromeda system has undergone past mergers — a giant stellar stream (the **Giant Southern Stream**) wraps around Andromeda, the tidal debris of a satellite galaxy consumed ~2 billion years ago.
