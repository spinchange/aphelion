# Australian Aboriginal Astronomy

The astronomical traditions of Australia's First Nations peoples — passed through oral transmission, ceremony, and art for tens of thousands of years. Often described as **"the world's first astronomers"**, Aboriginal Australians developed what may be the oldest continuous astronomical knowledge tradition in human history.

Most distinctively: where Western and Greek astronomy drew figures from the **bright stars**, many Aboriginal traditions drew figures from the **dark patches between stars** — the dust clouds and nebulae that interrupt the Milky Way.

## The Emu in the Sky

The most widespread Aboriginal constellation is not made of stars at all. The **Emu in the Sky** is composed of dark nebulae — opaque dust clouds visible as dark patches against the bright Milky Way background:

- **Head**: the **Coalsack Nebula** (beside the [[southern-cross|Southern Cross]])
- **Body and legs**: the Great Rift trailing through Scorpius
- **Neck**: connecting dark patches

The Emu is "almost everywhere" in Australian Aboriginal culture. Its visibility and orientation mark seasons for hunting and ecological activities. When the Emu appears lying on the ground (body low, legs trailing), emu eggs are being laid and can be found.

At Ku-ring-gai Chase National Park north of Sydney, a rock engraving by the Guringai people shows an emu in the same pose and orientation as the Emu in the Sky at a specific time of year.

## Stars as Social and Ecological Calendars

Aboriginal astronomy is embedded in ecology and social obligation rather than abstracted as pure geometry:

- **Pleiades rising at dawn (May)** marks the start of winter for the Pitjantjatjara → see [[pleiades-cluster]]
- **Orion appears** → dingo puppies about to be born (for some groups)
- **Scorpius appears** → Macassan fishermen will soon arrive to fish for trepang (Yolngu knowledge)
- **Lyra (Malleefowl) disappears in October** → time to gather malleefowl eggs on Earth (Boorong people)
- **Heliacal risings and settings** of specific stars tell groups when to move to new locations and what foods are available

The sky is a living calendar of animal behaviour and seasonal resources.

## The Yolngu Canoe — Reimagining Orion

Where the Greeks saw [[myth-orion|Orion the Hunter]], the Yolngu people of northern Australia see **Julpan** — a canoe. Three brothers went fishing; one ate a forbidden sawfish; the Sun-woman made a waterspout that lifted them into the sky.

- **Orion's Belt** (three stars in a line) = the three brothers
- **Orion Nebula** = the forbidden fish
- **Betelgeuse and Rigel** = bow and stern of the canoe

The same stars, the same night sky — radically different stories, each encoding ecological and ethical knowledge appropriate to their culture.

## Eta Carinae's Great Eruption — Recorded

In 2010, astronomers Duane Hamacher and David Frew showed that the Boorong Aboriginal people of northwestern Victoria **witnessed the Great Eruption of Eta Carinae in the 1840s** (when this otherwise-invisible star briefly became one of the brightest in the sky) and incorporated it into their oral traditions as *Collowgulloric War*, wife of *War* (Canopus). This is the only confirmed indigenous oral record of Eta Carinae's outburst in the literature.

## Wurdi Youang Stone Arrangement

Near Little River, Victoria, a stone arrangement by Aboriginal people may have been used to **predict solstices and equinoxes** — aligned with the setting Sun at the solstices and equinox. Its age is unknown but its astronomical precision has been documented by archaeoastronomers.

## Connection to the Pleiades

Aboriginal Australian traditions about the Pleiades (seven sisters) are among the cultural traditions cited as evidence for the **100,000-year-old Pleiades observation hypothesis** — that the "seven sisters" imagery across culturally unconnected traditions may encode a genuine ancient memory of when Pleione was visible as a seventh distinct star.
