# Polaris

The current North Star — the star that sits closest to Earth's rotational axis in the northern sky, making it appear nearly motionless while all other stars circle around it. Humanity's most used navigational star for the past thousand years. **But it wasn't always the pole star, and it won't be forever.**

## Why Polaris Is Special

Polaris lies nearly in a direct line with Earth's rotational axis. It currently sits **0.66°** from the celestial north pole — close enough that it traces only a tiny 1.3° circle in the sky over 24 hours, appearing essentially stationary to the naked eye.

**Latitude from Polaris**: measure Polaris's altitude above the horizon = your approximate latitude in degrees. At 35° altitude, you are at ~35°N. Corrections for the small circle it traces exist but are minor. This relationship is the fundamental tool of northern celestial navigation.

"Polaris always stays within 1 degree of the celestial north pole." — *Celestial Navigation*, Wikipedia

Columbus corrected for "the circle described by the pole star about the pole" on his 1492 trans-Atlantic voyage.

## The Wandering Pole — Precession

Earth's axis is not fixed. It wobbles like a spinning top, tracing a circle through the constellations over **26,000 years**. This is the **precession of the equinoxes**, which also causes the vernal equinox to drift through the zodiac over millennia.

The consequence: **the pole star changes**.

| Era | Pole Star | Approximate Date |
|---|---|---|
| Age of Egyptian pyramids | **Thuban** (Alpha Draconis) | ~2750 BC |
| Classical antiquity | **Kochab** (Beta Ursae Minoris) | ~1000 BC |
| Viking Age to now | **Polaris** (Alpha Ursae Minoris) | ~AD 500 → 3000 |
| Future | **Gamma Cephei** | ~AD 4000 |
| Far future | **Deneb** | ~AD 9100 |
| Cycle completes | Returns near Polaris | ~AD 27,800 |

The Greek navigator Pytheas (~320 BC) "described the celestial pole as devoid of stars" — Polaris was then ~3° from the pole, barely useful. Julius Caesar had no constant northern star; Shakespeare's "constant as the northern star" is therefore an anachronism. By Caesar's time, Kochab (Beta Ursae Minoris) was closer to the pole than Polaris.

## Polaris Is Also a Star

Beyond its navigation role, Polaris is a **triple star system** — a Cepheid variable supergiant (Polaris Aa) orbited by two companions. Research reported in *Science* suggests Polaris is **2.5 times brighter today than when Ptolemy observed it** ~2,000 years ago — an unexplained brightening 100× larger than stellar evolution models predict.

It appears on the flags and coats of arms of Nunavut (Canada), Alaska, and Minnesota — a pole star for three northern jurisdictions.

## Southern Equivalent

The Southern Hemisphere has no bright star near its pole — the closest is Sigma Octantis (barely visible at magnitude 5.4). The [[southern-cross|Southern Cross]] serves as the southern navigation reference, pointing to the pole without marking it.
