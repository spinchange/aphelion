# Astrolabe

The Swiss Army knife of medieval astronomy — an instrument that could measure star altitudes, determine latitude, tell time, find the direction of Mecca, predict sunrise and sunset, cast horoscopes, and teach spherical trigonometry, all in a handheld brass disc.

Etymology: Greek *astrolábos* — "star-taker." Arabic: *al-Asṭurlāb* (also *sitāreyāb*, Persian for "taker of the stars").

## What It Does

An astrolabe is a two-dimensional projection of the celestial sphere — a portable, analogue model of the sky. Its key components:

- **Tympanum**: a flat disc engraved with the celestial coordinate system for a specific latitude — horizon, azimuth lines, altitude circles
- **Rete**: a rotating skeletal overlay with pointers marking the positions of ~50 bright stars
- **Alidade**: a sighting bar on the back for measuring star altitudes

**To find latitude**: measure the altitude of a known star; compare with the rete to determine your latitude.

**To tell time**: measure the altitude of the Sun; set the rete; read the hour.

**To find qibla (direction of Mecca)**: calculate using the latitude disc and position of the Sun at the time of prayer.

Al-Sufi (10th century) catalogued **over 1,000 uses** for the astrolabe across astronomy, navigation, surveying, timekeeping, and religion.

## Origins and Development

The plane astrolabe was known in the Hellenistic world — Ptolemy may have used one. It was preserved in Byzantine scholarship, then taken up by Islamic astronomers who added angular scales, refined the mathematics, and made it a practical navigation instrument.

**First Islamic astrolabe**: Muhammad al-Fazari (late 8th century).
**Earliest known surviving example**: dated AH 315 (927–928 CE).
**Geofrey Chaucer** wrote *A Treatise on the Astrolabe* (~1391) for his son — the oldest surviving technical manual in English.

## Navigation at Sea

Standard astrolabes were difficult to use on a heaving ship deck. Islamic navigators developed the **Mariner's astrolabe** — a simplified, heavier instrument with the tympanum removed, designed to be held by a ring and allowed to hang vertically. Used to measure solar noon altitude and thereby latitude. Portuguese navigators carried mariner's astrolabes on their voyages of exploration in the 15th–16th centuries.

The **balesilha** (simplified astrolabe for sailors) was promoted by Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460) for Portuguese exploration.

## Transmission to Europe

Brought to Europe via Al-Andalus in the 10th century — possibly first introduced north of the Pyrenees by Gerbert of Aurillac (who became Pope Sylvester II). Its arrival drove Latin scholars' renewed interest in both mathematics and astronomy.

The astrolabe was the primary precision astronomical instrument for ~800 years, from the Islamic Golden Age through the Age of Discovery, before being superseded by the sextant in the 17th–18th centuries.
