# Space Debris

Defunct human-made objects in Earth orbit that no longer serve a useful function: derelict spacecraft, abandoned launch stages, mission-related debris, and fragmentation debris from breakups and collisions. A growing hazard to operational spacecraft and the long-term usability of orbital regimes. Referenced in [[geospace]] as a major threat to near-Earth space activity; created in part by the anti-satellite weapons permitted under the [[outer-space-treaty]].

## Scale (as of 2025)

- **40,230** artificial objects tracked by Space Surveillance Networks (ESA, April 2025)
- **~128 million** pieces smaller than 1 cm estimated in orbit
- **~900,000** pieces 1–10 cm
- **~34,000** pieces larger than 10 cm

Objects larger than 10 cm can be catastrophically destructive. Objects 1–10 cm are too small to track reliably but large enough to disable a spacecraft. Below 2,000 km, debris is denser than natural meteoroids.

## Kessler Syndrome

Proposed by NASA scientist Donald J. Kessler in 1978: if debris density in low Earth orbit (LEO) exceeds a critical threshold, collisions generate more debris, increasing collision probability in a runaway cascade. The distribution of debris could render certain orbital bands economically unusable for many generations.

The **2009 Iridium 33 / Kosmos 2251 collision** — the first major satellite collision — produced thousands of fragments at ~11.7 km/s closing speed. Both satellites destroyed. Two bands of LEO (900–1,000 km and 1,500 km) are already considered past critical density by the US National Academy of Sciences.

## Sources

- Fragmentation from satellite and rocket body breakups (explosions, battery failures)
- ASAT weapon tests (2007 Chinese test; 2019 Indian test)
- Satellite collisions (Iridium 33 / Kosmos 2251)
- Surface erosion (paint flakes, solid rocket exhaust)
- Frozen coolant from Soviet nuclear-powered satellites (RORSAT)

## Impact on Operations

- ISS performs avoidance maneuvers when collision probability exceeds 1-in-10,000; 16 maneuvers in the first 15 years of operation
- Space Shuttle required executive-level approval if catastrophic impact probability exceeded 1-in-200
- Small debris causes sandblasting damage to solar panels and optics
- Whipple shielding protects pressurized modules but cannot protect external surfaces

## Mitigation

Debris persists in LEO for decades to centuries depending on altitude — atmospheric drag is the primary natural removal mechanism below ~600 km. Above that, debris can remain for centuries. Active debris removal technology is under development but not yet operational at scale. International guidelines (but not binding law) recommend deorbiting LEO objects within 25 years of end of mission.

The problem is a **negative externality** — launch operators do not bear the full cost of debris they create, making international coordination difficult.
