# Crossing: Mercury / Hermes

**The messenger god who can reach everyone — mapped to the planet that is, on average, closest to everything.**

This is an **accidental crossing**: the mythological characterisation was not derived from the orbital geometry, yet it captures a structural truth about the planet that astronomy couldn't formalise until 2019.

## The Astronomical Fact (2019)

Stockman, Monroe & Cordner, "Venus is not Earth's closest neighbor," *Physics Today* (2019):

Mercury is the **closest planet, time-averaged, to every other planet in the Solar System.**

Not just to Earth — to Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. To all of them simultaneously.

The reason: Mercury is tethered to within ~28° of the Sun. It never wanders far from the solar centre. Venus can approach Earth to 38 million km but spends enormous spans of time on the far side of the Sun, ~250 million km away. Mercury's average distance to Earth over a full cycle is consistently smaller than Venus's, or any other planet's. The same logic applies for every planet — Mercury is the one body that is never very far from anyone.

## The Mythological Fact (Bronze Age)

[[myth-hermes|Hermes]] is the **messenger of the gods** — the one deity who can reach all others, cross all boundaries, appear in any realm. His core characteristic is universal accessibility. He alone can move freely between Olympus, the mortal world, and the Underworld.

"Due to his mobility and his liminal nature, mediating between opposites, he was considered [the divine principle of connection and accessibility]."

He is the youngest Olympian, last to join — and yet the most present, always available, never fixed to one domain.

## The Crossing

The Greeks (and independently, the Babylonians who called Mercury "Nabu," their own messenger-god) assigned the **messenger archetype** to Mercury based on observable behaviour:
- It moves fast — ~47 km/s, the fastest planet
- It appears erratic — rising and setting in different places, sometimes morning star, sometimes evening star
- It is always near the Sun — the hub around which all planetary motion organises
- It seems to visit all parts of the horizon over time

None of these observations require knowing that Mercury is statistically nearest to all planets. Yet the characterisation they derived — the messenger who can reach anyone, who is always accessible — is precisely what the 2019 proximity result formalises.

The myth arrived at the astronomy through a completely different epistemic path and got it right.

## What the Ancients Could and Couldn't Know

| Fact | Knowable? | How |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury moves fast | Yes | Direct observation |
| Mercury is always near the Sun | Yes | Max elongation ~28°, directly observable |
| Mercury appears in all parts of the sky over time | Yes | Long-term tracking |
| Mercury is statistically closest to all planets | **No** | Requires heliocentric geometry + statistical time-average |

The Babylonians tracked Mercury from the 14th century BC. The Greeks associated it with Hermes. Independently, the Babylonians associated it with Nabu, their own messenger-god. The Norse with Odin/Woden (hence Wednesday). The Maya with a messenger owl to the underworld. The Hindu with Budha.

Cross-cultural convergence on the messenger archetype, from civilisations with minimal contact, all looking at the same planet.

## On Whether Pythagoras Might Have Known

Pythagoras and his successors were sophisticated enough to *intuit* that Mercury's Sun-adjacency had some deeper significance. Aristarchus proposed heliocentrism (~270 BC). If someone had combined heliocentric geometry with the insight that orbital proximity to the Sun implies proximity to everything else — they could have deduced the proximity result. But no surviving text suggests anyone made this explicit argument. The formal proof waited until 2019.

The gap between "intuitable in principle" and "formalised" is exactly two and a half millennia.

## Epistemic Status

`crossing-type: accidental` — the correspondence was not derived from the astronomical insight; it arrived independently through mythological characterisation of observed behaviour.

`knowable-in-antiquity: false` — the statistical proximity claim requires heliocentric geometry and a time-average calculation that was not available to ancient astronomers, even the most sophisticated.

`formalized: 2019` — Stockman, Monroe & Cordner.
