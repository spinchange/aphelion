# Crossing: Saturn / Cronus

**The slowest visible planet — mapped to the god of time, the harvest cycle, and the age that came before.**

This is the clearest and most obviously `observed` crossing among the classical planets. Saturn's 29.5-year orbital period was directly observable by anyone who tracked the sky across decades, and that slowness — that temporal enormity — directly suggested the god of time and the deep past.

## The Astronomical Character

Saturn takes **29.46 years** to complete one orbit of the sky — the slowest of the five naked-eye planets. In a human lifetime of 70 years, Saturn completes barely two full circuits. It moves so slowly that month-to-month it appears nearly stationary compared to Jupiter, Mars, or Venus.

In practical terms: if you mark Saturn's position tonight, then look again in a year, it will have moved only about 12° — less than the width of your hand held at arm's length. Compare to the Moon (moves its own diameter every hour), Mercury (weeks per constellation), or even Jupiter (about 30° per year).

Saturn is the **outermost** of the classical naked-eye planets. Beyond it: nothing visible without instruments. Saturn is the edge of the known cosmos.

## The Mythological Character

Cronus ruled during the **Golden Age** — the time before Zeus, before law, before human struggle. He is the god of what was, of deep time, of the primordial harvest cycle. He is depicted with a sickle (harvest) and scythe (time's cutting). His festival Kronia suspended social hierarchies — a ritual return to the equalised order of the past.

Most importantly: Cronus is the **boundary** between the Titans (the old order) and the Olympians (the new order). He is the last thing before Zeus — just as Saturn is the last thing visible before the void.

## The Structural Correspondence

| Saturn | Cronus |
|---|---|
| Slowest visible planet | God associated with deep time and the long past |
| Outermost visible body | Ruler of the age before the current order |
| 29.5-year orbit (near-generational) | Associated with harvest cycles and long-term order |
| Edge of the known sky | Boundary between old gods and new |

Ancient sky-watchers who tracked Saturn across decades would have found it natural to associate the slowest, outermost wanderer with the god who ruled from before — who moved most slowly because he belonged to an older, heavier cosmic order.

## Wednesday → Saturday

The days of the week encode the seven classical bodies in order of their ancient orbital periods, slowest first: Saturn (Saturday), Jupiter (Thursday), Mars (Tuesday), Sun (Sunday), Venus (Friday), Mercury (Wednesday), Moon (Monday). Saturn's day is Saturday — the beginning of the weekly sequence, the ancient boundary.

## Crossing Type

`observed` — Saturn's exceptional slowness was directly visible over years and decades of naked-eye observation. No instruments, no orbital mechanics required. The correspondence to time, the harvest cycle, and the primordial past flows naturally from the simple observation that Saturn moves slowest of all.
